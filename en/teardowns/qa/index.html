QA automation

The QA automation shelf collects eleven write-ups from opening up other people’s test-automation and cause-investigation tools. It is my day job, and it is also the shelf that has taught me the least.

First, what “QA automation” means. When you build a game or an app, someone has to press everything and check that it works: press a button, see whether the right screen comes up, confirm the value landed. A person doing that by hand is QA, and getting a machine to do the pressing is QA automation.

My own field, and the smallest harvest

Across the first five pieces, the techniques I wrote down as adopted number 4.5, fewer than on any other shelf. When I checked, only one had actually gone into my code; that story is in the 2026-09 check below.

My first thought was that I must be reading them badly, but that was not it. Hold the same problem long enough and everyone arrives at similar answers, the way two climbers meet at the same spots when a mountain has only a few routes up. What you take from that is “my route was right.”

Why the research still paid off

Two kinds of return came out of it.

The first is that a conclusion I held alone became a conclusion held by two. Some of my judgements were my own, with no way to know whether they were right. Then someone I had never met, at a different company, turned out to have reached exactly the same answer independently. A sample of one became a sample of two, which is worth a lot even with zero techniques transferred.

The second is that someone else’s principle became a ruler for my own work. One piece offered zero techniques to take. But when I measured my code against the principle its authors had set, I found that a defence I had built was not wired into any actual run. I had learned nothing new and still had something to fix.

“Already there” did not always mean the same thing

With two pieces, I read it as one thing: I had already arrived at the same conclusion. By the fourth piece, two different states were hiding under that phrase. Arriving at the same answer and writing the answer down without ever doing it are not the same.

I was caught by the second one. Something I believed I had done turned out to be something I had only written down as intending to do.

One permission changed the nature of the evidence

One harvest here has nothing to do with techniques. Checking whether a game’s drop rate matches what was advertised used to mean pulling hundreds of times and doing statistics, which was slow and ended in “probably right.”

When access to the underlying data arrived, the same check became one query and five seconds, and “probably” became certain. The vantage point changed, and with it the kind of evidence available.

In the fifth, I went to judge a detector and got caught by it

The first four were other people’s talks and articles. The fifth was the first time I pulled someone’s code and ran it against my own data.

I went to test whether their detector caught what it claimed. Run against my data, it flagged a pile of things that were fine. It was the wrong tool for the place I meant to put it, though nothing is wrong with the tool itself. Without measuring, it would have come straight in.

Of the 4.5 techniques I wrote down as adopted, one reached the code

About a month on, I opened my code to see whether what each piece said it would take had actually gone in (the 2026-09 check).

Writing “adopt” and putting it in turned out to be separate jobs. Marking a row “adopted” in a verdict table feels like the work is finished at that moment, and the code knows nothing about it. So this shelf’s number is now written as “4.5 of 4.5 applied” rather than “4.5 techniques.” It is the one that was in at the check plus the 3.5 put in over the next two days: a publish check that stops on an unknown kind of file, the contradiction check for being stuck, the two-clocks confirmation, and a long-form-only sentence-rhythm check.

The effects differ. The rhythm check caught two easy-layer passages on its first run and they were fixed. The two stuck-detection pieces have not filtered anything in simulation yet, because the current bot pushes the whole time it walks and nothing in the simulation inches forward. What went in and what has had an effect are counted separately too.

One mistake ran the other way. In the autoplay piece I said my stuck detection had only one layer; when I opened the code, a second layer was already there, and that piece now says so.

Outside this shelf, something built by someone else entered my QA pipeline for the first time: the evaluation model Jev, attached as the photo judge’s “second eye” (2026-09-24). It came in through a four-day trial rather than a teardown, and in the range measured it has not yet caught a real error. The details are in The photo judge.

The sixth piece took the same subject apart twice and never ran it

clumsy is a tool that makes a PC’s internet connection worse on purpose. I opened it for disconnect testing, and only after finishing did I find a report from August that had already reached the same conclusion. Both times I wrote “use it,” and neither time did I run it.

The check above taught me that writing something down and putting it in are separate jobs; this time I went through that twice inside a single subject. So the shelf’s number becomes “4.5 of 7 applied.” Clumsy adds 2.5 to what was written down and nothing to what went in.

The seventh tool helps investigate what a failed check means

REA lets an agent inspect executables and applications. I tried static analysis on a Windows binary and a small JavaScript fixture, then examined the boundary between findings and inference. Two exercised probes do not amount to a QA integration. The 4.5 / 7 adoption figure belongs to the earlier six pieces, and these probes are not added to that tally.

The eighth borrows one idea from a different road: proof

Claude formal verification is Anthropic’s work having Claude prove that 123 open-source libraries never crash under stated conditions. Proofs are not my job, so I took one idea from it, agents copying each other’s finished work, put it into my game studio’s art team, and measured before and after. Time fell 21% and cost stayed flat. This is not added to the 4.5 / 7 figure either.

The ninth lets an AI operate desktop programs through a parts list, and I put it on hold

agent-desktop reads and presses programs through the OS accessibility list instead of through screenshots. I read its docs and code, then tried it briefly on my PC and removed it. Reading worked, but I could not show it runs separately while I work. Android game screens publish no such list either, so I held it and took away one principle: tie a tag to the moment you looked. This is not added to the 4.5 / 7 figure either.

The tenth re-measures a number from someone else’s essay

Judged by the model, scoped by people is another QA engineer’s essay arguing that an agent should consult a judgment model before every action. I mostly had that structure. Re-asking the essay’s 0.57 looked more like a split between options than doubt, and applying that fix to my tests buried a bug. I kept one rule for writing questions, also left out of the 4.5 / 7 figure.

The eleventh lets an AI use a real smartphone, and it stopped at game screens

phone-harness lets an agent like Claude Code look at and tap an iPhone or Android phone the way a person would. I watched an introduction video and checked it against the repository code. Ordinary apps read well, but game screens are invisible to it too, and its picture-text recognition only runs on a Mac, so it is on hold. I took one principle, tagging every read value with where it came from, and left it out of the 4.5 / 7 figure.

Not because the teardowns were bad — because enough years under the same constraints tend to land you in the same place already.

An investigation that yields nothing isn’t wasted. Asleep turned a set of conclusions with a sample size of one into two — on-prem, fail-closed on live, unattended regression, element-based targeting. Nebula handed over zero techniques and instead surfaced that the official gateway’s defences aren’t wired into the path a real run takes.

And in both of the early ones, my own verdict got overturned

Asleep took two corrections. I wrote down “silence detection is a new harvest,” then deleted it because it was already in my code; then tried to close at “zero new,” and found that my excerpt had been too thin — the section the author calls the core wasn’t in it at all.

Nebula lost the only harvest in its first draft. I’d adopted an in-device controller on the strength of a real measurement — uiautomator dump at 2,350 ms — and then found that call appears zero times in my pipeline. A game client draws the whole screen into one native surface, so there is no accessibility tree to dump.

The measurement was right and the target was wrong. Before benchmarking anything, check whether the call actually happens in my run.

At four pieces, “already there” split into two meanings

At two I read it as one thing — “I’ve already reached the same conclusion.” At four, two different things turned out to be mixed together inside that.

One is genuinely the same place — ten of Asleep’s eleven decisions were that. The other looked like the same place and was one layer shallower. The autoplay piece was that. I was also judging when a bot is stuck, and I wasn’t wrong, I had simply never separated the layer that produces candidates from the layer that removes false positives.

You cannot tell these two apart without opening the thing, because both look like “we already do that.” The moment I split the layers, the cause of my false positives acquired a name — “a normal state where it stopped on purpose.”

(Correction, as of 2026-09) “Never separated the layers” was wrong for the autoplay piece. Since the July code, my stuck detection has had a second layer that confirms by trying to escape, behind the candidate (under 60 cm of movement in 3 seconds). What was one layer shallower is not the number of layers but the contradiction check that looks at input and movement together. That went in on 2026-09-28.

And this bucket holds one harvest that isn’t a tool

The infrastructure talk yielded zero techniques and left one case: verifying gacha probability. What used to be hundreds of pulls and a statistical estimate became one query in five seconds once read access existed — and the character of the evidence changed from estimate to certainty.

It didn’t get faster. What counts as evidence changed. It goes straight into the writing about verdicts, which makes it the only harvest from this bucket that turns into prose. (As of 2026-09 it went in: “Probably right” is not a verdict uses this case as a jump of several rows “from a sample to a direct read of the source”.)

The fifth was this bucket’s first repository, and I went to judge a detector and got caught by it

The first four were all somebody’s talk or article. watermarks-remover is the first piece here where I read code, which let me do the one thing only that allows — run someone else’s detector against my own data.

The result was the most expensive thing this bucket has produced. A metric built to separate AI writing flagged, on its first outing, something a human wrote — me. Sentence-length coefficient of variation across 98 long pieces on this site sits at a comfortable median of 0.668; move it to the drafts from the channel where the rule would actually live, and one in six falls under the threshold — and that one was already published.

Measure a false-positive rate in the wrong place and the rate itself is a lie. Calibrate on long-form, apply to short-form, and you are worse off than before you measured.

Read as QA this is a familiar failure: test data that doesn’t match the production distribution. Except this time I was the one who chose the test data. The Nebula piece taught me to ask “does this call actually happen in my runs”; this was the hole one seat over — the call does happen, but the sample I measured came from somewhere else.
