"Agent harnesses" examines the machinery around an AI agent: 24 analyses, each with its own adoption decision recorded in the piece itself. [Harness2](/en/teardowns/harness/harness2/index.md) was added on 2026-10-19, and [BrowserSkill](/en/teardowns/harness/browserskill/index.md), which had been missing from this index, was linked at the same time. The "21 pieces, 1 adopted" tally below belongs to its original period and is not a fresh count of what is installed today.

## What a harness is

Literally, a harness is the tack you put on a horse. An AI needs something similar, because the AI itself only exchanges words, and getting a job done means strapping machinery around it. One part tells it what rules to keep, one grants it tools, one makes it ask a person before anything dangerous, and one checks the work when it is done. That bundle is the harness: if the AI is the horse, the harness is the reins and saddle.

## It became the largest shelf

The ledger holds 24 harness analyses as of 2026-11-14. I did not design the categories first and fill them; I filled them, counted, and found this was the biggest. That number is where my time went.

## At twenty-one pieces, one tool had come in

When the shelf held twenty-one pieces, I had taken 21 apart and installed 1. Even that one was not installed for efficiency. I installed it because I wanted it, and I wrote that on its page, since memory later rewrites a choice into a rational one. Writing the reason down where it happened is the only defence.

## The twenty that stayed out had different reasons

I assumed it meant there was nothing good out there, but the rejection reasons sorted into kinds. Sometimes I already had the same thing. Sometimes it was good but nothing in my setup could take it. Sometimes it was downloaded and installed and would not run, and sometimes the vendor's own table contradicted its marketing. The third and fourth came up most often.

## The commonest outcome was owning it and never switching it on

At six pieces I saw only "no tools taken." By twenty, a more frequent conclusion had emerged. I first read it as "I already had this," but there was a layer beneath: I had it and had never once switched it on. Owning something is fine, while never running it is the same as not owning it, and a file listing makes the two look identical.

That is why this shelf reads differently from the others. Elsewhere the question is what I can take; here it is closer to looking at what I already run through someone else's eyes. Open another harness and the parts of mine I had stopped seeing, because they seemed obvious, come back into view.

## The refutation usually sat in the subject's own material

In eight pieces, the decisive evidence that overturned my verdict was in the target's own material, not in a critical article. It was a table in their marketing, a rule they wrote themselves that said stop when followed, a changelog containing what the download page left out, the second table of the same talk reversing its title's premise, two of their documents disagreeing, their own source breaking their own rules, one repository carrying three different numbers at once, and their own check failing to catch its own idioms when run directly.

What I took from this is that you rarely need to hunt for criticism. Reading the vendor's material to the end usually turns it up, because marketing is on the front page, the limits are on the back, and people read the front.

## Five of the twenty were picked by a machine

Up to fifteen I chose by hand. The five that took the count to twenty came from a feed that a collector I built gathers twice a day from six sources, and I took apart everything it had stacked up, in order, without filtering. It was the first time that route was actually used, so I was switching on something I had built and never used, and the shelf's commonest conclusion applied to me as well.

## The twenty-first was my own warehouse

The twenty-first piece took apart something I had installed myself 21 days earlier and never used. The first twenty were all other people's work, and the verdicts were "not taking it" or "already have it." Only in the twenty-first was the target mine.

## The detailed record starts here

Things that provide the **floor** an agent runs on: rule sets, skill directories, self-improving harnesses, hook dispatchers, model gateways. Someone else's operating system, opened up and held against my own.

At the time of the twenty-one-piece tally, this bucket held **twenty-one of forty-two subjects: exactly half**. The observations below preserve that period. The current count is 23; the old adoption ratio has not been relabeled as a current one.

## Which changes how this bucket is read

The other buckets get read as "what can I take." This one is closer to **"look at what I'm already running, through someone else's eyes."**

ECC is the extreme case. It wasn't someone else's repo — it was **the upstream of my own configuration**, copied out as files six months ago and never synced since. That was the first time the drift got measured.

Two more landed in the same place. In Odysseus, **of five disciplines the docs declare, the code enforces one.** In ponytail, the investigation turned up not their rule set's problems but **three contradictions inside mine.** Open somebody's operating system and what you usually see first is your own.

## The twenty-first was my own warehouse, not someone else's

[eli5](/en/teardowns/harness/eli5/index.md) is the first piece in this bucket where the subject was **something I had already installed myself**. The first twenty are records of opening other people's work, and the verdict was either "not coming in" or "I already had this." The twenty-first was something I installed with my own hands 21 days ago **and never once used**.

So the numbers in that piece measure my usage, not the subject's performance — 944 session logs carried its name while the recorded run count was 1. **That analysis did not change the period's "1 adopted" tally.** eli5 did not come in through a teardown; it was already in. What changed is not adoption but **the path by which it gets called**. Put those two in the same column and the adoption rate quietly inflates.

## The second line — "I already had this"

At six, only one pattern showed: no tools come in. Six more, and **a different conclusion turns out to be the more frequent one — "I already had this."**

Cross-session messaging is the extreme case. The mechanism the video dug up doesn't exist on my OS at all, and yet **the capability I actually wanted was already switched on.** ECC wasn't someone else's repo but the upstream of my own config. The ponytail investigation surfaced not their rule set's problems but **three contradictions in mine.** Even rejecting all seven of OmniRoute's techniques comes down to the same axis — harvesting a piece means keeping the original open in front of you, and **there is no reason to do that for something I can already do.**

So the work I do most often in this bucket isn't evaluating someone else's thing. It's **taking inventory of my own.**

### At twenty, this line gained a layer

**Not "I already had this" but "I have it and have never once switched it on."**

I went into a harness with 181,170 stars meaning to take away five techniques, and all five died against **two skills already sitting in my own folder with zero run history.** One leaves no output anywhere in six months of records; the other's log file and trash folder — both of which it instructs you to create — **do not exist.** It never ran.

This wasn't a one-off. The same skill turned up again in [the context-budget piece](/en/teardowns/memory/dsh-anchored-standard/index.md): installed 24 days ago, run zero times. **Two investigations that knew nothing about each other pointed at the same object.**

The unit of the inventory was wrong. **Count only "what do I have" and you conclude you should go buy the thing that is already in the warehouse.** What needs counting is not the list but **the date it was last switched on.**

## The third line — the target was holding the tool that broke it

At fifteen, something else came into view. **In four pieces, the decisive evidence that overturned a verdict came from the subject's own material.** Four of the five new ones did it again, so it is **eight** now.

| Subject | Where the decisive evidence sat |
|---|---|
| [Four plugins](/en/teardowns/harness/four-plugins/index.md) | **the promoter's own table** |
| [obra/superpowers](/en/teardowns/harness/superpowers/index.md) | **their own rule** — "if the control shows no failure, stop." So I stopped |
| [ASIDE](/en/teardowns/harness/aside/index.md) | **their own changelog** — what the download page lacks appears three times |
| [One skill, ad images](/en/teardowns/harness/payg-image-routing/index.md) | **the second table in the same talk**, which reverses the title's premise |
| [ip-as-logo](/en/teardowns/harness/ip-as-logo-skill/index.md) | **its own two documents disagree** — the headline rule is in the README and not in the executable file |
| [anti-slop](/en/teardowns/harness/anti-slop/index.md) | **21 lines of its own source**, which break four of its own rules |
| [DSH better-sidebar](/en/teardowns/harness/dsh-better-sidebar/index.md) | **the same repository carries three different numbers** at once |
| [cumora](/en/teardowns/harness/cumora/index.md) | **its own CI guard** — call it directly and it catches none of the repo's own idioms |

There is one exception. Only [DeepSeek Harness](/en/teardowns/harness/deepseek-harness/index.md) had its decisive evidence outside — a 404 from the package registry and community reports. Even that **was not a critique.** Nobody commented; a machine answered.

**There was no need to go looking for outside criticism.** If anything, the outside criticism was wrong twice — a quoted figure eight months stale, and a verdict of stagnation that hadn't looked at the branch layout.

So the adversarial pass in this bucket has a fixed order now. **Before finding out what anyone else said, check whether what the subject said about itself hangs together.**

## Five arrived at once, and this time I didn't pick them

Up to fifteen I picked by hand out of a link library. **The five that took this bucket to twenty came from a feed a machine scrapes every day** — [Just out](/en/ai-issue/) walks six sources twice a day, and I took apart every GitHub item it had stacked up, in order, all of them. This is the first time that path was actually used.

Changing who picks **changed what the sample is.** When I pick, I pick things that look like they touch my problems, and the verdicts converge on "I already have this." When the feed picks, **things unrelated to my problems come in** — one of the eight was Apple-only and could not be run here at all, so it [was not published](/en/teardowns/) — and in exchange, **places I would never have opened get opened.** Three of the five found defects in my own setup, and all three were subjects I had no reason to pick.

The verdicts came out harsher, not softer. **Of the five: zero adopted, one deferred, four rejected.** Stars don't carry usefulness with them — add up the five and it clears 190,000, and the number of tools that came in is zero.

And one thing the ledger can't show. **Prime Agent has two reports** — the repository analysis and a video. The ledger counts subjects rather than files, so it's one row; and the piece above was written from reading the code, so **nothing from the video is in it yet.** That's the kind of fact a table can't hold, so it goes here.
